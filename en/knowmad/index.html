3// El Corte Inglés — catalogue domain.
4// This is where Kafka stopped being a CV line: a price change
5// propagates without any service calling another.
6@Component
7@Slf4j
8@RequiredArgsConstructor
9public class CatalogueEventConsumer {
10
11    private final LearningRepository learnings;
12
13    @KafkaListener(topics = "catalogue.product.updated",
14                   groupId = "catalogue-service")
15    public void onEvent(CatalogueEvent event, Acknowledgment ack) {
16
17        log.info("Event from {} for SKU {}",
18                 event.client(), event.sku());
19        try {
20            learnings.saveAll(Learning.extractFrom(event));
21            ack.acknowledge();          // manual offset commit
22        } catch (Exception e) {
23            log.error("Failed processing {} → DLQ", event.sku(), e);
24            throw e;
25        }
26    }
27}

# Knowmad Mood · Nov 2022 – Sep 2025

Three years of consultancy for two clients: BeeDigital primero (online business positioning, on AWS) and El Corte Inglés afterwards (the catalogue domain, MongoDB and a lot of volume).

This is where microservices stopped being "what you are supposed to use" and became something I actually understood: why they are split, when it pays off, and what happens when one goes down. Also where Kafka went from a line on the CV to an everyday tool.

The concrete case: any change to a catalogue product —a new price, a new item— has to reach every service that shows it, and in that domain there were around 150 of them talking over Kafka. Solving it with service-to-service calls couples them, and one service going down drags the rest with it; published as an event, each consumer handles it at its own pace, commits the offset once it has actually finished, and whatever fails ends up in a separate queue instead of being lost.

The previous stage (NTT Data) and the current one (Sopra Steria) have their own chapter.
